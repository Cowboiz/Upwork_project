# Business Model

> Version: v0.1  
> Status: Hypotheses for validation, not final commercial decisions.

## Marketplace Thesis

Students and student-led teams regularly need small, time-sensitive digital work but often lack the skill, network, confidence, or budget to hire through a traditional agency or navigate a large freelance marketplace.

At the same time, early-career designers and developers need real project opportunities, portfolio evidence, client experience, and income, but struggle to win work on broad platforms where reputation is already concentrated among established freelancers.

A focused marketplace can create value by narrowing both sides:

- Student-sized projects.
- A limited set of digital project categories.
- Vetted providers.
- Structured project briefs.
- Manual curation at the beginning.
- Fast matching instead of complex bidding.

The core marketplace hypothesis is that **better curation and lower friction can create enough trust and liquidity to outperform generic discovery channels for small student projects**.

## Customer Segments

### Demand: Students

- **Who pays?** Primary hypothesis: the student, student founder, club, or project owner who requests the work.
- **What budget range is realistic?** Initial hypothesis: approximately USD 50–500 equivalent for the first project categories, with most pilot projects targeted around USD 100–300 equivalent. Local pricing should be validated before launch.
- **What project types are most urgent?**
  - Landing pages and small websites.
  - UI/UX mockups and prototypes.
  - Portfolio or club sites.
  - Hackathon or startup MVP slices.
  - Branding and design assets.
  - Small technical fixes, integrations, or automations.
- **What purchase objections are likely?**
  - “Can I trust the provider?”
  - “Will the work be finished before my deadline?”
  - “Is this price fair?”
  - “What happens if the result is poor?”
  - “Can I explain the project clearly enough?”
  - “Why should I use this instead of a friend, social group, Upwork, or Fiverr?”

### Supply: Designers and Developers

- **Who provides services?** Early-career designers and developers with portfolio evidence and enough experience to complete clearly scoped small projects independently.
- **What rates or payout expectations are realistic?** Working hypothesis: project-based payouts of roughly USD 50–400 equivalent for the initial categories, depending on scope. The pilot should validate category-specific economics instead of fixing hourly rates.
- **What skills are required?**
  - Web design and UI/UX.
  - Front-end or full-stack web development for small scopes.
  - Graphic/brand design.
  - Prototyping.
  - Simple integrations and automations.
- **What makes them trust the platform?**
  - Real, reviewed leads.
  - Budget and deadline transparency.
  - Clear scope before work begins.
  - Low proposal overhead.
  - Fair dispute handling.
  - No hidden fees.
  - Respect for provider time.

## Value Proposition

### For Students

- **Faster access to capable project help:** Submit one structured brief and receive a curated next step instead of searching across many profiles.
- **Clearer pricing:** Use budget bands and scope clarification before matching.
- **Lower risk:** Vet providers, keep basic operator notes, clarify deliverables, and record project outcomes.
- **Better communication:** Give both sides a concise brief, expected timeline, scope, and contact process.

### For Providers

- **Qualified leads:** Only send opportunities that match stated skills, availability, project type, and budget.
- **Faster payment:** In later stages, use a reputable payment provider; in validation stages, record whether and when payment occurs without storing card data.
- **Reduced sales effort:** Avoid mass bidding; providers respond only to curated relevant opportunities.
- **Portfolio or experience growth:** Encourage legitimate completed-work evidence, reviews, and repeat relationships where permitted by the client.

## Revenue Model Options

These should be tested before committing to a final model.

- **Commission per completed project:** Strong long-term fit because revenue scales with marketplace success. Hypothesis: 10–15% total platform take rate. Requires payment handling and increases dispute/chargeback complexity.
- **Fixed listing or matching fee:** Simple and predictable but creates friction before the platform proves a match will occur.
- **Student service fee:** Could be 5–8% on top of the project price once payment is integrated. Risk: students are price-sensitive.
- **Provider subscription:** Better after the marketplace has reliable lead volume. Weak fit for an early marketplace with uncertain demand.
- **Managed service margin:** Platform quotes the student and pays the provider, potentially retaining 20–30%. Higher revenue potential but much heavier operations and delivery responsibility.
- **Other:** Optional premium brief-scoping or priority-matching services may be tested later, but should not distract from the core marketplace.

### Recommended Validation Sequence

1. **Stage 1:** Do not require a platform fee. Validate demand, provider response, matching, and real willingness to pay for the underlying project.
2. **Stage 2:** Test a transparent transaction fee with a small subset of successful matches.
3. **Stage 3:** Integrate payment only after repeated paid matches justify the complexity.

## Initial Pricing Hypotheses

- **Typical project price range:** USD 50–500 equivalent.
- **Target pilot sweet spot:** USD 100–300 equivalent.
- **Minimum viable order value:** Hypothesis: USD 50 equivalent; below this, matching and support overhead may exceed value.
- **Platform take rate or fee:** No mandatory fee in the first manual validation; later test 10% total take rate before considering higher fees.
- **Refund or dispute reserve:** Do not hold funds directly in the manual pilot. If payment integration is introduced, reserve/refund behavior should follow the payment provider and a documented dispute policy.

## Acquisition Channels

### Student Acquisition

- **Campus communities:** University clubs, entrepreneurship societies, hackathon communities, coding/design clubs.
- **Social groups:** Founder groups, student Facebook/Discord/Telegram communities, alumni communities.
- **Paid ads:** Not a priority until an organic message converts; use only for controlled experiments.
- **Partnerships:** Student clubs, incubators, university entrepreneurship programs, hackathon organizers.
- **Referrals:** Offer a simple referral loop after the first successful projects.

Priority hypothesis: founder-led outreach and community partnerships should come before paid acquisition.

### Provider Acquisition

- Design/dev communities.
- Student freelancers.
- Junior talent networks.
- Bootcamp or university alumni.
- Portfolio communities.
- Referrals from high-performing providers.

Provider onboarding should be selective enough to protect quality, not optimized for raw sign-up volume.

## Marketplace Liquidity

- **Minimum supply needed for launch:** 20–30 vetted, responsive providers before promoting the service broadly.
- **Minimum demand needed for launch:** 10–15 qualified requests during the first pilot, scaling toward 20+ for a useful signal.
- **Expected match time:** First response within 12 hours; curated shortlist or next step within 24 hours; target accepted match within 48 hours.
- **Initial geographic, language, or project-type constraints:** Start with one founder-accessible university/community cluster, one primary operating language, and 2–3 project categories. Exact geography and language remain an open founder decision.
- **Category concentration:** Prefer categories where at least 5 responsive providers are available before actively acquiring demand.

## Validation Experiments

| Experiment | Hypothesis | Method | Cost | Success Metric | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Landing-page demand test | Students have a real problem worth submitting | Simple landing page + project request form + direct outreach | Low | 20 qualified requests in 4–6 weeks | Continue if qualified requests arrive without heavy incentives |
| Concierge matching test | Manual curation can produce useful matches | Operator reviews each request and introduces 1–3 providers | Low | >=50% of qualified requests reach a viable match | Automate only repeated steps |
| Real-price test | Students will pay meaningful amounts | Present real provider quotes and require a real paid scope | Low | >=30% of matched students agree to pay; >=5 paid completions | Continue if willingness to pay is real |
| Provider activation test | Providers value curated student leads | Recruit 30 vetted providers and send relevant opportunities | Low | >=60% of contacted providers respond; >=30% accept at least one suitable lead | Refine category/provider mix if weak |
| Referral test | Successful users create organic growth | Ask completed users for referral or repeat request | Very low | >=20% refer, repeat, or explicitly intend to reuse | Build referral features only if behavior exists |
| Fee sensitivity test | Marketplace can capture value | After successful manual matches, offer a transparent 10% fee variant to a small cohort | Low | Majority of successful users accept or show low resistance | Validate monetization before payment integration |

## Risks

- **Low willingness to pay:** Students may like the idea but have budgets too small for reliable service. Mitigation: validate real prices early and narrow to higher-intent project types.
- **Poor provider quality:** A small number of bad outcomes can destroy trust. Mitigation: portfolio review, limited initial supply, manual matching, outcome tracking.
- **Trust and safety issues:** Users may misrepresent identity, scope, or work. Mitigation: basic verification, clear rules, operator review, documented escalation.
- **Academic integrity concerns:** Requests may involve graded coursework or prohibited assistance. Mitigation: explicit prohibited-work policy, request-screening question, refusal/removal process.
- **Payment disputes:** Scope ambiguity can cause refund or non-payment disputes. Mitigation: written scope, deliverables, price, deadline, revision expectations before work begins.
- **Seasonality:** Demand may spike around hackathons, semester deadlines, or club cycles. Mitigation: track request source and season before extrapolating growth.
- **Marketplace leakage:** Student and provider may move off-platform. Early stage: accept this as a learning signal; later create enough value in payment, trust, and workflow to justify staying.
